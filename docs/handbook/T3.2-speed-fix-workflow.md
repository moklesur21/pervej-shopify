# T3.2 — The Speed-Fix Workflow
**Module 3 · Tutorial 15 of 26 · Est. 5–6 hours · Closes Module 3**

**Goal:** Turn diagnosis into results — the fix playbook ranked by real-world yield, the measurement discipline that makes gains *provable*, and the **before/after report** that closes S1 deals. The drill: sabotage a theme with a full set of performance crimes, then fix it commit by commit with attribution.

**Prerequisites:** T3.1 complete — the eight passes and Template 4 are today's raw material.

---

## 1. Concept

### The workflow

```
1 Baseline capture → 2 Ranked fixes (one per commit, re-measure each) → 3 Function QA → 4 After capture → 5 Report
```

**1 — Baseline, before touching anything.** Lighthouse lies in single runs — throttling variance, extension noise, CDN mood. The discipline: incognito window, mobile throttling, **three runs per page, record all three, take the median.** Home + product minimum, screenshots archived, git tag on the untouched state, field-data snapshot if the store has traffic. No before photo, no proof of value — and the proof *is* the deliverable.

**2 — Fix in ranked order, one fix per commit, re-measure after each meaningful change.** This is the step most developers skip and the one that makes you expensive: when the report says *"image surgery bought 1.9 s of LCP; the font trim bought 0.3 s,"* that attribution is (a) credibility, (b) future quoting wisdom, and (c) only possible if you measured between fixes.

**3 — Function QA.** The QA checklist's "performance must not regress" has a mirror: performance fixes must not regress *function*. Full purchase-flow test after surgery, console clean, every time — a fast store that can't add to cart is a two-regression-rule strike, not a win.

**4/5 — After capture, same method, same environment; then the report.**

**Environment consistency — the silent credibility killer:** localhost, preview links, and live URLs all produce different numbers. Before and after must come from the **same environment**; the client-facing pair should come from a shareable one (theme preview or live), never from your laptop's localhost.

### The fix playbook, ranked by typical yield

**A. Image surgery — usually the headline win.**
- Every rendered image requested at slot size: `image_url: width:` + `image_tag` with sane `widths`/`sizes`.
- **The LCP image gets royal treatment:** never lazy, preloaded (`preload_tag`) or `fetchpriority: 'high'` passed through `image_tag`.
- Everything below the fold: `loading: 'lazy'`. Everything: `width`/`height` present (CLS insurance).
- A Shopify-specific relief: the CDN transforms *any* original into the right size and format **when asked correctly** — so unlike Woo, a merchant's 4 MB upload is survivable and the fix is almost entirely code-side. You fix the *requests*, not the library.

**B. App-bloat surgery — the honest scope.**
- **Embed toggles:** switch off app embeds for apps the store doesn't actively use — *with client sign-off* (conversation-level, per T3.1's triage; the app's owner is the client, not you).
- **Ghost-code removal:** hunt departed apps' leftovers — orphaned snippets, `{% render %}` calls to nothing, stale script tags in `theme.liquid`. Comment out → test → delete → commit, one ghost at a time.
- **Know your limits:** a live app's own embed internals are its vendor's code — you can't optimize what the platform injects for them. Your levers are toggles, ghosts, placement (app blocks only load where placed), and the recommendation to replace heavy apps. Say that plainly in scoping and you'll never over-promise.

**C. Font trim.** Cut to the families and weights actually used; serve from the theme's font system (Shopify CDN), preload the single critical face, confirm `swap`. Third-party font origins are removal candidates on sight.

**D. JS discipline.** `defer` everything deferrable; delete dead theme scripts; per-section loading (Module 2 doctrine applied backwards to inherited code); hunt duplicate libraries — hacked-on themes routinely carry two sliders and one and a half jQuerys.

**E. CSS.** Calibrate expectations: on Dawn-shaped themes, CSS is already split per component and rarely the win. On mangled themes, remove unused stylesheet loads. Chase images and JS first; touch CSS when evidence points there.

**F. Liquid render cost (occasional).** Slow TTFB on *every* page points at expensive loops in global sections — a mega-menu iterating products, unpaginated ambition. Cap loops, precompute via menus or metaobjects. Rare, distinctive symptom, cheap fix.

**G. CLS.** Dimensions on all media and embeds, reserved space for banners and late-injected app UI, and calm font swapping. Named shifting elements from the T3.1 pass become a punch list.

### Template 5 — the before/after report (`speed-report.md`)

```markdown
# Speed optimization report — [store] · [date]
Method: Lighthouse, mobile emulation + throttling, incognito, median of 3 runs
per page, identical environment before/after ([preview URL]).

## Results
| Page | Metric | Before | After | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Home | LCP / CLS / INP / Score | … | … | … |
| Product | LCP / CLS / INP / Score | … | … | … |

## What we did, and what each fix bought
1. [Fix] → [metric movement] — [one-line why]
2. …

## Field-data note
Real-user (CrUX / Shopify Web performance) figures trail lab fixes by up to
~28 days of rolling window — expect the dashboards to catch up over the month.

## Recommended next steps (not in this scope)
[conversation-level items: app removals/replacements, section rebuilds…]

## Screenshots
[before/after pairs, linked]
```

Two rules give this document its power. **Honest numbers:** medians, never a cherry-picked lucky run; a metric that didn't move gets said out loud — trust is the product, same family as the two-regression rule. **The next-steps section is your pipeline:** every conversation-level finding you didn't fix is the seed of the next quote.

## 2. Woo bridge

| Your W4-era ritual | Here | The shift |
|---|---|---|
| Install WP Rocket, toggle options, remeasure | No magic-plugin phase exists | Every gain is *authored* — which is why attribution is affordable and impressive |
| Plugin deactivation sweep | Embed toggles + ghost-code hunt | Same sweep, new switchboard, plus sign-off etiquette |
| Smush/regenerate-thumbnails campaigns | Mostly unnecessary — CDN transforms | Fix the requests, not the library |
| Critical-CSS plugins & tooling | Rarely needed on component-split themes | A whole subgenre retired |
| "It's the hosting" escape hatch | Gone | Front-end truth only — and that's leverage, not loss |
| Measure once at the end (admit it) | Measure between every fix | The report's spine, and the habit that separates tiers |

## 3. Claude Code drill — sabotage, then surgery

You'll receive a "client theme" — from yourself. Committing crimes teaches their signatures; fixing them under measurement discipline teaches the job.

### Part A — Duplicate and sabotage (~30 min)

From `dawn-playground`'s main: branch **`t3-2-client-slow`** — the duplicate-before-touching rule, practiced on yourself. Then commit the crimes (have Claude Code inject them from this list — speed matters, care doesn't, for once):

1. **LCP crime, double:** the homepage hero requested without a width (master size) *and* given `loading="lazy"`.
2. **Gallery crime:** two more slots stripped of width params and `width`/`height` attributes.
3. **Script crimes:** in `theme.liquid`'s `<head>`, non-deferred script tags to two heavyweight public CDN libraries the theme never uses (an old jQuery is traditional), plus a small inline script that busy-loops ~300 ms on load (long-task simulator). One more script tag pointing at a plausible-but-dead app path — your ghost.
4. **Font crime:** a third-party fonts link pulling two extra families at five weights.
5. **CLS crime:** a promo banner injected by JS *after* load with no reserved space.
6. *(Optional)* **Liquid crime:** a snippet in the layout nesting loops over collections × products.

Commit: `"as received from client"`. Tag it.

### Part B — Baseline discipline (~30 min)

The broken state gets the full treatment: incognito, mobile throttle, **three runs each** on home and product, all runs recorded, medians computed, screenshots saved, consistent environment noted. You will *see* the variance between runs — that's the lesson single-run auditors never learn.

### Part C — Mini-audit and the ranked plan (~30 min)

Run the T3.1 eight passes on your own crime scene — they should run fast now. Produce the ranked fix plan (impact × effort). Predict, in writing, what each fix will buy: calibrating your predictions against reality is how quoting instincts are built.

### Part D — Surgery (~2 h)

Branch `t3-2-fixes` off the crime scene. The loop, strictly: **one fix (or one tight group) → review protocol → commit → re-measure (3 runs, median) → log the attribution line.** Work your ranked order — expect image surgery first and expect it to headline. Claude Code implements under your plan; the never-break rules and the parallel-styles hunt stay in force.

Close surgery with **function QA**: full purchase flow with a test order, drawer, tabs, filters — everything Module 2 built still works, console clean.

### Part E — The report and the retro (~45 min)

Fill Template 5 completely: results table, per-fix attribution (predictions vs actuals — keep both), the field-data note, next-steps (the optional crime you didn't fix belongs there), screenshots. Then the retro: which fix over/under-delivered vs your prediction, and by how much?

Label the finished document **"method sample — training simulation"** and keep it presentable: it honestly demonstrates the S1 method and report format to prospective agencies until real client reports replace it. *(Stretch, worth it: run the whole cycle once on the Horizon playground — T1.6's mobile-gap note becomes personal data.)*

## 5. Done when

All boxes ticked **and** you can answer out loud:

1. Why three runs and a median — and what breaks in your credibility without it?
2. What does one-fix-per-commit buy that batch-fixing never can?
3. Name the app-surgery levers you *have* — and the one you don't, and how you say so in scoping.
4. Why is a merchant's 4 MB upload survivable here when it wasn't on Woo?
5. What two jobs does the next-steps section of the report do?
